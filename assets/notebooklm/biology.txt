What is measurement?
• How do we know that a biological system has changed?
e.g. Bacterial growth
“the bacteria have grown” is a qualitative observation.
If we say:
“The bacterial population increased from 100- to 150 cells/mL in 12
hours,” we have made a measurement
What is measurement?
• Measurement
• The process of assigning a numerical value to a property of a biological
system using a defined method and appropriate standard or reference.
• Biology: cell number, biomass, pH, metabolites, DNA/RNA, proteins,
enzyme activity, rates
• Key question: What exactly is being measured—and what does the
signal represent?
Qualitative vs quantitative
Qualitative: It describes what is present or what has happened.
'culture is turbid', 'colonies are red', 'cells are motile'
Quantitative: It tells us how much.
OD600 = 0.85; 2.5 × 108 CFU/mL; glucose = 2.5 g/L
Quantification enables comparison, modelling and statistical inference
Why measure biology?
• Observe → Quan fy → Compare → Analyze → Predict
• Measurement turns a biological observation into testable evidence
• Applications: microbiology, environmental biotechnology, diagnostics,
fermentation, ecology
What can be measured?
• Physical: mass, volume, temperature, pressure, density
• Chemical: pH, concentration, dissolved oxygen, metabolites
• Cellular: number, size, biomass, viability, membrane potential
• Molecular: DNA, RNA, proteins, metabolites
• Functional: growth rate, respiration, photosynthesis, enzyme activity

Errors in biological measurement
Instrumental: drift in spectrophotometer, poor calibration, defective
sensor.
Experimental: pipetting, dilution, mixing, timing, contamination
Biological: natural cell-to-cell and sample-to-sample variation
Human/data handling: reading, calculation and transcription errors
Random vs systematic error
Random error → fluctuate around actual value → mainly aﬀects
precision
Systematic error → consistent bias → mainly aﬀects accuracy
Random error can often be reduced by replication; systematic error
requires calibration/correction
Sensitivity and limit of detection
Sensitivity: ability to detect a small change in analyte
Limit of detection (LOD): lowest quantity reliably distinguishable from
background
Critical for toxins, hormones, biomarkers, pathogens and heavy metals
Specificity and selectivity
Specificity: ability to measure the target analyte despite other
components
Biological samples are chemically complex
A highly sensitive but non-specific method can still produce misleading
results
Calibration
Calibration establishes the relationship between instrument response
and known standards.
Example: glucose standards → absorbance → calibra on curve →
unknown concentration.
Recalibration is needed when instruments drift or conditions change

Replication: technical vs biological
• Technical replicates: repeated measurements of the same biological
sample
• Biological replicates: independent biological samples
Technical replicates assess measurement consistency; biological replicates
capture biological variability

• Normalize only when the normalization variable is biologically justified

LAB MANUAL SECTION FOR THIS LECTURE'S EXPERIMENT:
1. BMI and its correlation with blood pressure (Biological Sciences)
Aim: measure height, weight and blood pressure with replicates, classify the data types, compute BMI from
averaged replicates, and find the Pearson correlation coefficient r between BMI and BP.

Instruments and principles
Stadiometer – height from heel plane to the top of the head (vertex), in m. Barefoot, heels and back
against the board.
Digital scale – weight via strain-gauge load cells, in kg. Check it reads 0.0 kg first.
Digital sphygmomanometer – the oscillometric method: senses arterial-wall vibrations as the cuff
deflates. Systolic BP = peak pressure during ventricular contraction; diastolic BP = minimum pressure
during ventricular relaxation; in mmHg. Cuff at heart level, subject seated and rested for 3 min, 1 min
between readings.

BMI

Underweight < 18.5 · Normal 18.5–24.9 · Overweight 25.0–29.9 · Obese ≥ 30.0
Replicates: three readings each (step off and back on between readings). Mean of replicates cancels
random error; the replicate range (max − min) shows trial-to-trial precision.
Limitations of BMI: doesn’t separate muscle from fat (athletes may show “overweight”), ignores fat
distribution (visceral belly fat is the riskiest), ignores differences of age, sex and ethnicity. It’s a
screening tool, not a diagnosis.
Data types: subject label – nominal; BMI category – ordinal; temperature in °C – interval; height, weight,
BMI, BP – ratio.
