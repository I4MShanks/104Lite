The process of surveying is therefore in three stages, namely:
(i) Reconnaissance
This is an exhaustive preliminary survey of the land to be surveyed. It may be either ground reconnaissance or
aerial reconnaissance survey. Reconnaissance is made on arrival at the site, during which an overall picture or
view of the area is obtained. The most suitable station positions are selected, the purpose of the survey and
the required accuracy are determined, and finally the method of observation is established.

(ii) Observation and Measurement
• This part denotes the next stage of any survey, which in land surveying constitutes the measurement to
determine the relative position and sizes of natural and artificial features on the land. Linear measurements
primarily involve measuring distances between points.
• Angular measurements which deal mainly with measuring angles between survey lines.
• The angle can be either in the horizontal plane or in the vertical plane.
• Other measurements include: heights, gravity at a station, temperature, etc.
(iii) Presentation of Data:
• The data collected in any survey must be presented in a form which allows the information to be clearly
interpreted and understood by others/end users. This presentation may take the form of written reports, Job
Record Jackets (JRJs), drawings, maps, and plans, etc., showing the features on the land.

Uses of Surveying
• Surveying may be used for the following purposes:
• To prepare a topographical map which shows the hills, valleys, rivers, forests,
villages, and towns.
• To prepare a cadastral map which shows the boundaries of fields, plots, houses and
other properties.
• To prepare an engineering map which shows the position of engineering works, such as
buildings, roads, railways, dams, canals, etc.
• To prepare a contour map to know the topography of the area to find one of the best
possible sites for roads, railways, bridges, reservoirs, canals, etc.
• Surveying is also used to prepare military maps, geological maps, archaeological maps,
etc.
• For transferring details from the map on the ground.

Plane Surveying
Plane Triangle
• Earth surface is considered as a plane
• Curvature of the earth is ignored
• Line joining any two stations is considered to be straight

Geodetic Surveying
• Curvature of the earth is taken into account
• It generally extends over larger area
• The line joining any two stations is considered as a curved line
• The triangle formed by any three points is considered as spherical and
the angles of the triangles are considered to be spherical angles
• Carried out for a large area exceeding 250 km2

Surveying Based on Instruments
Classification of Surveying
• Classification based on Instruments:
• Chain Survey: This is the simplest type of survey in which only linear
measurements are made with a chain or a tape.
• Compass Survey: In a compass survey, angles are measured with the
help of a magnetic compass.
• Chain and Compass survey: In this survey, linear measurements are
made with a chain or a tape and angular measurements with a
compass.
• Plane Table Survey: It is a graphical method of surveying in which
field work and plotting are done simultaneously.
Classification of Surveying
• Theodolite Survey: In theodolite survey, the horizontal angles are measured
with a theodolite more precisely than compass and the linear measurements are
made with a chain or tape.
• Tachometric Survey: A special type of theodolite known as tachometer is
used to determine horizontal and vertical distances indirectly.
• Leveling Survey: This type of survey is carried out to determine the vertical
distances and relative heights of points with the help of an instrument known
as level.
• Photogrammetric Survey: Photogrammetric is a science of taking
measurements with the help of photographs taken by aerial camera from air
craft.
• EDM Survey: In this type of survey, all measurements are made with the help
of EDM.

Classification of Surveying
Classification based on methods
• Triangulation: Triangulation is a basic method of surveying. When
the area to be surveyed is large, triangulation is adopted. The entire
area is divided into a network of triangles.
• Traversing: A traverse is a circuit of survey lines. It may be open or
closed. When the linear measurements are done with a chain and tape
and the directions or horizontal angles are measured with a compass or
a theodolite respectively, the survey is called traversing.

4. Height of a building with a theodolite (Civil Engineering)
Aim: find the height of a building by trigonometric levelling: a theodolite measures the vertical angle to
the top and a tape measures the horizontal distance.

D = 25.50 m, h = 1.45 m, θ = 32°15′ (tan = 0.6310): H = 1.45 + 25.50 × 0.6310 = 17.54 m.
Temporary adjustments: set up the tripod, centre over the station with a plumb bob, level with the
plate levels and foot screws, focus to remove parallax.
Read the vertical angle on Face Left and Face Right and take the mean – this cancels the index error of
the vertical circle and collimation errors.
Take 2–3 sets and average.
Why add h? D tan θ only gives the rise above the trunnion axis. Forgetting h is the commonest
beginner’s error.
Error in D propagates as ≈ ΔD tan θ – worse at steep angles (close to a tall building).
Sources of error: poor levelling, parallax / mis-bisection / index error, wrong D (sag, temperature, nonhorizontal tape), wrong h, sighting a different point each time, refraction over long distances.
Least count: vernier theodolite ~20″; digital 1″–5″.
