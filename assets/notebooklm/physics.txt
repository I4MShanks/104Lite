▶ Enhanced oil recovery:

▶ A bulk molecule is
surrounded symmetrically ⇒
net cohesive force ≈ 0.
▶ A surface molecule has
neighbours only below and to

▶ The surface behaves like a
stretched elastic membrane,
always minimising its own
area.

work done

Capillary rise: Setting up the force balance
▶ Narrow capillary (radius r )
dipped into a wetting liquid.
▶ Liquid climbs the inner wall,
meeting glass at contact
angle θ.
▶ Surface tension acts along
the contact circle (length
2πr ), tangent to the liquid
surface.
▶ Only the vertical
component, γ cos θ,
supports the column.

Uncertainty in the Web-App Technique
Each h comes from four taps (measurement: bulk level, meniscus; calibration:
two reference points) – but unlike the vernier’s LC, per-tap placement error
cannot be assigned a single quantified value: it mixes device resolution,
lighting, image sharpness, and the individual student’s finger/stylus precision.
So measure the uncertainty directly, instead of propagating it:
For each liquid, repeat the full measurement (photograph → calibrate
→ tap) 5 times, independently. Compute the mean h̄ and standard
deviation σh of the 5 readings; take δhapp = σh .

Take-home message
A simple force balance – surface tension against gravity
– gives a quantitative, testable measurement of a
fundamental liquid property, and the same property
governs phenomena from breathing to microfluidic chip
design.
Go measure it carefully. Happy learning.

Capillary bore: I.D. 0.55 ± 0.10 mm (catalogue); verified r = 0.275 mm, δr ≈ 0.0035 mm (δr/r ≈ 1.3%) –
found as a difference of two microscope readings, so δd = LC/√2 ≈ 0.00707 mm and δr = δd/2.
Techniques:
Graph paper fixed on the tube (or a metre scale, LC 1 mm): δh ≈ 0.5 mm.
Web-app image method (this semester): photograph, calibrate with two taps on a known graph-paper
length (pixel → mm), then tap the bulk level and the meniscus. No least count can be defined, so
measure 5 times and take δh = standard deviation – a Type A uncertainty.

Travelling microscope (LC 0.01 mm): h = R_top − R_bottom, so δh = LC/√2 ≈ 0.00707 mm (not
simply half the LC).
SDS is a surfactant (hydrophilic head, hydrophobic tail); it collects at the surface and lowers γ (≈ 43
mN/m vs 72.8 for water). That’s why soaps and detergents work.
Literature values at 20 °C: water 72.8 mN/m (ρ 998), 25% glycerol ≈ 71, 50% glycerol ≈ 66 mN/m.
Density changes with temperature: ρ(T) ≈ ρ₂₀ − β(T − 20).

Precautions: clean capillary held perfectly vertical (tilt is a systematic error not covered by the LC), dip
only ~1 cm and don’t touch the walls, read at eye level (parallax), record the actual liquid temperature,
gloves for SDS, a dedicated capillary for each liquid.
If a result is far off, incomplete wetting (θ > 0) may be a more likely cause than instrument error.

Water
h = 54.0 mm, ρ = 998 kg/m³, r = 0.275 mm: γ = 0.0540 × 998 × 9.81 × 2.75 × 10⁻⁴ / 2 = 0.0727 N/m.
Graph paper (δh = 0.5 mm): δγ/γ = √(0.93² + 1.3²)% = 1.6% → γ = 72.7 ± 1.2 mN/m. With a travelling
microscope δh/h ≈ 0.013%, so the radius term dominates (1.3%).
